## Platform Screen Doors

Platform screen doors (PSDs), platform edge doors (PEDs), and platform gate doors (PGDs) are being discussed in New York City following [the death of Michelle Go, who was pushed onto the tracks at Times Square](https://www.nytimes.com/2022/01/15/nyregion/woman-pushed-on-train-death.html). Platform screen doors, platform edge doors, and platform gate doors provide a barrier between individuals and the tracks, helping prevent people and objects from falling onto the tracks. The three styles differ in height with platform screen doors extending from floor to ceiling, platform edge doors extending from floor to near the height of the ceiling, and platform gates extending from floor to either waist or chest height. Platform screen doors can then be further subdivided into two categories, vertical platform screen doors and horizontal platform screen doors, depending on whether the doors open vertically or horizontally.

Each of these approaches have different costs and benefits, and I would like to discuss these and explain why I believe the NYC Metro should build vertical platform screen doors at all underground stations, and either horizontal platform screen doors or platform gate doors at all above ground stations.

Creating a barrier between the platform and track generally serves three key purposes:
1. Keep people off of the tracks, either do to jumping or being pushed.
2. Prevent objects, including litter, from being thrown on the tracks. These debris often catch on fire from electrified third rails. These are not as rare as people think, and [occur between 300-500 times per year](https://www.nydailynews.com/new-york/ny-mta-subway-track-fires-20210804-lfpq5vs57nhxvnbdxk4k4zhnfe-story.html).
3. Improve climate control within the station by preventing climate controlled air from dispering out of the stations via the tracks.

The cost aspects that need to be considered are:
1. Cost of installing PSDs / PEDs / PGDs
2. Cost of ensuring PSDs / PEDs / PGDs are fitted properly considering different train dimensions and train door dimensions and locations.

In regards to the first benefit, all of the three platform door methods help, although PSDs and PEDs do a better job since someone would be able to climb over a PGD. For platform gate doors, chest height doors does make it more difficult for people to end up on the tracks than waist height doors. For the second benefit, PSDs and PEDs again do a better job than PGDs, since individuals can still throw litter over a PGD. For the third benefit, PSDs provide better results than PEDs and PGDs because it allows the platform to be completely separated from the tracks, not allowing air to transfer between. PEDs then provide better results than PGDs because they do greatly limit the air that travels between the platform and tracks, although it does not completely eliminate the air transfer.

At this point, PGDs seem to be significantly worse at solving the problems that these different platform door methods address when compared to PSDs and PEDs. This is true; however, PGDs have the lowest cost of the three methods. When would it make sense for the MTA to implement PGDs instead of PSDs and PEDs? At outdoor stations, climate control is currently not a point of focus (although sometimes it seems to not be a point of focus at indoor stations either). This already means that the differentiation between PSDs and PEDs is not significant. Then, if the station is not very crowded or tends not to have much litter on the tracks, it could make sense to implement PGDs rather than PEDs. 

PGDs provide a significant cost benefit in outdoor stations for multiple reasons:
1. Less material needed since doors are approximately half the height of PSDs and PEDs.
2. PSDs require both a lower and upper area of attachment in order to create a fully enclosed platform area. Similarly, PEDs reach a near-ceiling height in order to provide maximal benefits, and so it would also require a roof. This means a roof will be required to extend along the entire platform, and so the cost of roofs will also need to be included in building out PSDs. 

The one PGD concern, which is a concern for all platform doors that open horizontally, is what happens when the platform allows trains of different rolling stock, which may feature a differnet number of doors per side, different door widths, and different rolling stock lengths. There are two possible solutions:
1. For stations that service multiple types of rolling stock that differ in the above dimensions, build vertical platform screen doors as part of the PSD method that will be described below.
2. Consider all varieties of doors per side, door widths, and rolling stock lengths, and build extra-wide PGDs and potentially PGDs at different locations to handle all potential door placements. This is not ideal as PGDs wider than rolling stock door width will cause slower headway times, and excess doors (and generally excess moving parts) lead to wasted resources, such as electricity.

A myth I would also like to briefly mention is that many individuals think horizontal PSDs, PEDs, and PGDs require CBTC, but this is not the case. [In the Paris Metro Line 1, the platform screen doors operate independent from the CBTC.](https://www.clearsy.com/wp-content/uploads/2021/08/PSD-control-systems-DOF-and-COPPILOT-January-2021.pdf)

If cost is a serious concern in building platform doors (and cost is very often a concern for the MTA), I believe it would want to use PGDs at outdoor stations, and following the second approach outlined above.

Indoor stations should be prioritized in regards to implementing Verticals Platform Screen Doors, or VPSDs. Vertical platform screen doors have one major issue and many benefits. The issue is speed - VPSDs take longer to open and close, which can make a difference during peak hours when headways can be as little as one minute (and that number will only become smaller with the implementation of CBTC). Since VPSDs have to travel the height of the station, which ranges between 7.41 and 8.37 feet, rather than the width of the door, which varies between 4.17 and 4.50 (soon to be 4.83) feet, it naturally takes longer to open and close. However, the benefit is that it would be able to handle the variety of rolling stock.

The older rolling stock, which will be the next to get phased out, currently have a significant number of cars. It would be unrealistically expensive to upgrade all rolling stock to ones that fit the same dimensions (even if we allowed for multiple dimensions, one for the A Division and one for the B Division), in order to use the better Horizontal Platform Screen Doors. For this reason, Vertical Platform Screen Doors should be implemented and future rolling stock should drive towards uniform dimensions, and eventually the NYC Metro would be able to replace the Vertical PSDs with Horizontal PSDs. That upgrade would be costly and should not be considered high priority, but implementing the first wave of VPSDs and PGDs should be seen as a priority for the MTA.

[Recently, the Sofia Metro in Bulgaria implemented VPSDs at multiple stations, demonstrating that this approach is possible.](https://www.railwaypro.com/wp/sofia-metro-benefits-automatic-platform-screen-doors/) The Sofia Metro currently has three varieties of rolling stock: Metrovagonmash 81-717/81-714, Metrovagonmash 81-740/741, and Siemens Inspiro, each with different dimensions, yet the VPSDs have been successful.

Clearly, the New York Metro has a lot of room for improvement regarding Platform Screen Doors, which currently do not exist (although it does exist in the AirTrain JFK people mover). The death of Michelle Go is tragic, and we should focus on ensuring these racially motivated attacks, as this appears to have been, do not happen (along with other attacks too). We should also examine what else can be done to make the NYC Metro safer. With Vertical Platform Screen Doors at Times Square station, this attack would have still hurt Michelle Go, but she would most likely still be alive. Platform Screen Doors, Platform Edge Doors, and Platform Gate Doors increase safety and provide additional benefits, and should be an area of priority for the NYC MTA.

So based on this report, what should the MTA do? On page 3 of the report, the MTA wrote "YCT plans to complete the procurement of new rolling stock, per division, with nearly identical car geometries/door spacing, by 2033. Accordingly, STV’s assessment assumes homogenized car classes, per division, by the year 2033." Assuming there are no issues regarding rolling stock, it will still need to make changes to stations according to the issues described in the report. I believe the MTA should be aggressively addressing these issues so that when the new rolling stock is ready in 2033, platform screen door implementation can begin.

The 9 services that have the highest percentage of feasible stations are, in order:
1. Franklin Avenue Shuttle
2. A/C services
3. L service
4. Times Square Shuttle
5. E service
6. 7 service
7. G service
8. Q service
9. J/Z services

With the exception of the Q service, that list comprises of the shuttle service (the Rockaway Park Shuttle was included in the A/C service analysis), the A/C/E services, the L service, the 7 service, the G service, and the J/Z services. It would make sense to pilot platform screen doors on the shuttle services, and then expand the pilot to the 7 service and L service, followed by the G, A/C/E, and J/Z services.

The most obvious services to work on are the two shuttle services - each with a single non-feasible station. On the Times Square Shuttle, Grand Central Station is the problematic station, and has the following description:
"At the western end of the North platform the existing columns are 16 inches from the platform edge. That creates a conflict at the platform edge that make half height APGs and full height PSDs impossible. Above these columns there is an existing low girder that would make installation of full height PSDs even more difficult without reframing the existing roof structure."
On the Franklin Avenue Shuttle, Prospect Park Station is the problematic station, and has the following description:
"The width of the platforms at the north end is 3’-10”. The implementation of a platform edge barrier would reduce this width below the required minimum of 36”. The remaining 31” or less* would not allow for ADA compliant wheelchair movement."

Even without platform screen doors being implemented until 2033 (of course, this is not when it would be implemented, but when it could theoretically be implemented due to the new rolling stock), it seems reasonable for the MTA to begin work on improving stations for structural support and accessability. ADA issues account for 43% of stations being infeasible, structural integrity of pre-cast platforms accounts for 23% of stations being infeasible, and columns being too close to the edge accounts for another 8% of stations being infeasible.

On the A/C/E services, 62.22% of problematic stations are due to ADA issues and none of the problematic stations are due to the structural integrity of pre-cast platforms or columns being too close to platform edges. Below is an example of platform screen doors causing an ADA issue:

![Image of a platform screen door causing an ADA issue]({{ site.baseurl }}/assets/images/psd_ada_issue.png)

This is common amongst the stations with ADA issues - support columns are near staircases, walls, or some other physical object and divide up a pathway into a section not wide enough for a wheelchair to pass. Addressing ADA compliance from added platform screen doors would make over 50% of stations feasible on all services except for the 1/2/3 and 4/5/6. This should be one of the first areas to address (along with columns being too close to the platform edge) because it will improve safety, mobility, and accessability on stations by improving areas where people are able to walk or wheelchair. The services mentioned previously (Shuttles, A/C/E, L, 7, G, J/Z) still remain the most feasible. If you also include addressing the issue of columns being too close to the platform edge, the N/Q/R/W then has a higher percentage of stations feasible than the L.

The next major area of focus is improving the structural integrity of stations with pre-cast platforms. The report states:
"Structural considerations; existing pre-cast panels which are typically found at elevated platforms cannot support the added load of PSDs / APGs. The installation of PSDs at precast elevated platforms was a subject of analysis in the Structural Feasibility Report of April 2018 (See Appendix B). As noted there, PSDs would require full replacement of the platform thus changing the scope of a PSD project from an Alteration 2 to an Alteration 3 and triggering a full seismic upgrade to the existing station structure. Such extensive work would not be in proportion to the benefit."

Although this is understandable, it is still disappointing because platform screen doors will greatly increase the quality of service. I will still briefly discuss this improvement. Once the issues relating to ADA accessability and columns being too close to the platform edge are addressed, all issues on the J/Z services related to structural integrity of pre-cast platforms, as well as approximately 90% of issues on the 7 service. This issues generally impacts elevated stations. The report provides details regarding existing platform structures beginning on page 2816 (appendix B, section 4.0). In subsection 4.3, it describes precast concrete platforms at elevated stations. I will include the entire description here, as it is very insightful:

"Starting around 1960, the wood platforms at elevated stations were replaced with predominantly precast concrete slabs. About 70% of elevated platform structures consist, at least partially, of precast concrete slabs. These are typically double-tee beams supported by the steel track structure below. The overall width of the beams varies, but the stems are typically 3’-0” apart. Some of the precast beams are prestressed and contain prestressing tendons in addition to mild reinforcing steel. The stems of the tees are connected to short pipes, which are welded to the steel platform girders below. Between stems, the slab is fairly thin, about 3 ½” thick, and reinforced with welded wire fabric. See Figure 4-3 for a typical detail of a prestressed platform slab.

While the overall design of precast concrete platforms varies from station to station (typically, multiple stations were completed under one contract with the same details), the precast concrete platforms generally cannot support the design loads of a PSD system. The thin slab is not capable of withstanding the rotation created by a cantilever PSD system, as it will experience torsional forces between stems. As a result, any precast beam will require some degree of reinforcement, largely driven by the spacing of the stems. Additionally, the steel station structure and pipe supports for the precast beams must be analyzed on a site-specific basis for added weight and additional imposed loading. The reinforcing of precast concrete platforms is discussed in further detail in Section 5.0.

The PSD system may also present logistical challenges in a precast structure, as the base connections and necessary penetrations for conduits may interfere with existing reinforcing or prestressing steel. A cast-in-place concrete slab allows for the use of post-installed adhesive anchors at base connections or for the slab to be rebuilt with base connections cast into the slab. This is not possible with a precast concrete slab, as the use of post-installed anchors would be precluded by the thin slab. The only viable option for a base connection is the use of thru-bolts, which may be challenging, as the stems and existing reinforcement must be avoided. Installation of PSDs at elevated stations with precast concrete platforms will present substantial challenges, possibly necessitating major modifications to the existing structures.

Full-height PSD systems are likely precluded from use at nearly all elevated stations, as they do not have canopy structures running the full length of each platform to support the top of the barrier. If a full-height barrier is to be used, it would have to be cantilevered in a similar way to the half-height barriers and would produce a greater base reaction at the platform slab edge.""

This also brings up an additional reason for infeasability for platform screen doors, but not platform screen gates, which is that the canopy needs to extend across the entirety of the platform. This was mentioned in my initial article, and was mentioned in the report, but was not listed on a station-by-station level as a reason for infeasability, due to the fact that the primary issue at each of those stations is the pre-cast platforms, and also that it does not completely prevent platform screen doors, but makes them less useful and more difficult to implement.

Once stations are modified for ADA compliance, removing columns too close to the edge, and reinforcing pre-cast stations, the shuttles and the J/Z services will all be feasible. It must be noted that once these changes are implemented, there will need to be further tests and analyses on stations to ensure no other issues arise. On the 7 service, the only station that will still not be feasible is the Court Square Station because there is no room for PSD related equipment. There are two ways to address this issue:
1. Create a new area to store the equipment. This will be expensive due to construction of a new area to store the equipment, and may not be feasible.
2. Decrease the train length by one car.
Decreasing train length decreases capacity, so some measures will need to be implemented to increase capacity again. Platform screen doors, in addition to CBTC which alreasy exists on the IND Flushing Line, will already lead to increased capacity, as trains will be able to enter and exit the platforms quicker, especially on the 7 express service, which will not have to slow down as local stations as much as it currently does. Furthermore, there will be less delays due to less track fires caused by debris on tracks. There are also several improvements that can be made with the rolling stock aside from the dimensions, since train width could lead to potential issues along the track. The next generation of rolling stock could have 4 or even 5 sets of doors per side instead of the current 3 doors per side, allowing for easier onboarding and offboarding, further decreasing time at stations. Once this issue at Court Square Station is resolved, the 7 service will be feasible for PSDs, but will need further analyses before proceeding.

The A/C services will also only require one station to be updated once the previously discussed improvement are implemented. At the 168th Street Washington Heights Station on the north end of the southbound platform, implementation of a platform screen door would cause the corridor for evacuation to be under the threshold that the MTA uses.

Our station egress analysis (attached as Appendix C) finds that 5’-11” is a minimum platform width which will not impede egress with an installed PSD system."

Addressing this issue would require either re-designing or somehow moving the car equipment cleaning station; however, without more detailed information about the structure of the station and the internal design of the car equipment cleaning station, it is difficult to provide an approach to resolving this.

The G Train at this point would have one station not feasible due to having no space for a platform screen door equipment room at Fort Hamilton Parkway Station. Unfortunately, the report did not include details about this station (it included details about the Fort Hamilton Parkway Station on the N service and the Fort Hamilton Parkway Station on the D service though). I will assume that with enough creativity and effort to re-design, expand, or re-organize the station, the MTA will be able to find space for this room.

On the L Train, the two stations not feasible due to having no space for the equipment room are the Bedford Avenue Station and the Lorimer Street Station. The two stations not feasible due to egress spacing issues are the New Lots Avenue Station and Rockaway Parkway Station. Once these issues are addressed, the only stations that would have problems along the shuttles, the A/C/E, J/Z, L, 7, and G services will be due to door alignments. 

On the 8-car G trains, there will be train door misalignments caused by shared track with 10-car F trains. The conductor cabins on the G trains will align with standard cabins on the F trains. This issue could be alleviated if train door alignments were slightly modified on future F train rolling stock (and all future rolling stock) so that train door alignments are consistent between standard cabins, conductor cabins, and work trains. This would also resolve this issue on the E service in track it shares with the M service. Fixing these door alignment issues on future rolling stock would then resolve all currently identified issues on the G service and A/C/E services. 

Fixing a non-compliant egress at the 72nd Street Station on the B service and Coney Island Stillwell Avenue Station on the D service, as well as finding space for Platform Screen Door Equipment Rooms at Union Street Station, 9th Street 4th Avenue Station, Prospect Avenue Station, 25th Street Station, and 36th Street Station on the D service will resolve all currently identified issues on the B/D/F/M services.

Following this, there will still be stations on the 1/2/3, 4/5/6, and N/Q/R/W services that would require upgrades, mostly to create space for egresses and platform screen door equipment rooms, but also adjusting 14th Street Union Square Station due to currently implemented gap fillers that would need to be modified (and possibly removed, which would lead to other changes needing to be made). 

The MTA should aggressively purseu resolving all issues not relating to rolling stock so that when we reach 2033 with newer rolling stock, implementation can be tested at several stations and then future implementation can occur swiftly.
